1. What this system is for

You have a planner spreadsheet with one row per trip. Some rows already say which bus runs them. Many do not. The job is to give those trips a bus without paying for empty driving — a bus that finishes a trip in Copenhagen should, if at all possible, pick up its next trip from Copenhagen a day or two later.

Doing that by hand across hundreds of rows is what this app replaces. The loop is:

  1. Import your xlsx (or csv) on the Data tab.
  2. Look at what the app proposes — on Suggestions (as text) or Timelines (as a calendar).
  3. Accept the proposals you agree with. Only accepting writes anything.
  4. Export the xlsx back out and send it on.

Six tabs carry the whole app: Data (the sheet itself, and every correction you make), Suggestions (the plan in words, with the accept controls and the bulk actions), Timelines (the same plan as a calendar), Worklist (assignments broken by a cancellation), Registry (which companies, buses and cities exist), and Manual — this text.

Three promises worth knowing up front

Nothing is ever assigned automatically.
The app plans, ranks and explains. A human accepts. Even with Show plan on buses ticked on the Timelines board — which only changes where the plan is drawn — nothing reaches your spreadsheet until you press an Accept button.
Your file is never rewritten, only patched.
The export is your original workbook with only the cells this app wrote spliced into it. Formatting, comments, formulas, the legacy date grid at the far right, and every row the app could not read are preserved exactly. Export a file you have changed nothing in and you get the same bytes back.
Bad data is reported, never repaired.
A row with an unreadable date or an unknown city is not guessed at and not dropped. It lands in the Non-operable table with the reasons listed, it can be fixed there, and it is passed through to the export untouched in the meantime.

What it is not. It is not a costing tool (there are no distances or day rates in the data, so it cannot price an empty run — only count them), not a seat-capacity planner, and not a repair tool for the legacy columns of the sheet.

2. Getting started — your first half hour

2.1 Start the app

From the project folder: 

.venv/bin/python -m buschain.web

Then open http://127.0.0.1:8000. It runs on your machine; there is no login and no server anywhere else. Your work is stored in buschain.db next to the project, so closing the browser loses nothing.

2.2 Import the workbook

On the Data tab press Import xlsx/csv and pick your planner file. The app reads the trips from the first tab of the workbook, whatever that tab is called — it gets renamed each season, and the name does not matter — with the header on row 1. If some other tab sits in front of the trips (a notes sheet, a chart, or a hidden tab), the import says so, names that tab and lists the others; drag the trip tab to the front in Excel and import again. The header row is checked either way, so the app will refuse a sheet whose columns have shifted rather than read every value one slot off. The Information sheet is found by its name and can sit anywhere after the trips.

Importing replaces the current working dataset, so you are asked to confirm. Anything you had changed in-system survives only in exports you already downloaded.

2.3 Seed the registry (once)

Straight after the first import, almost every row will be non-operable and a yellow banner will say so. That is expected: the app does not yet know which companies and city codes are real, and Departure, Arrival and Company name all have to resolve against that list.

Press Seed from Information sheet — either in the banner or on the Registry tab. It reads the workbook's Information sheet and builds the company list, the numbered units, and the city codes. The non-operable count should collapse.

Read the seed warnings. They appear under the button and name every company the seeder refused to guess at (an alias like “Nordi Bus”, a fleet number it could not match). An unread seed warning is exactly how two real buses once went unregistered and took 31 otherwise-good rows out of the working set. Add the missing entries by hand on the same tab.

2.4 Read the contract summary

At the right of the Data toolbar you will see something like 1 612 operable · 58 non-operable. Those two numbers are the whole health check: operable rows can be planned, non-operable rows cannot until their reasons are fixed.

2.5 Look at the plan

Open Suggestions. You should see a summary line — “113 of 190 uncovered trips can be chained onto 24 units…” — and a card per bus underneath. Nothing has been written; these are proposals.

If it says instead that there is nothing to plan inside the plan-ahead window, the season starts further out than the planner is looking: Plan ahead defaults to one month. Widen it (chapter 6.4).

2.6 Accept, then export

Accept a chain, a single link, or the whole plan (chapter 6), then go back to Data and press Export xlsx. The downloaded file is your original workbook with the assignments filled into the Company name column.

3. The ideas the whole app is built on

Six ideas explain nearly every screen. They are worth ten minutes now because every message in the app is phrased in them.

3.1 Demand and supply

A demand is a live trip (Confirmed or Pending) whose Company name cell is empty — it needs a bus.

Supply is the arrival of a trip that is assigned to a numbered bus. When Kautra 3 finishes a trip in Copenhagen on 5 July, that is supply: a bus, in a place, from a date.

All the app ever does is connect arrivals to departures.

3.2 A numbered unit is a bus; a bare company is not

Value in Company nameMeaningCan it chain?
Ollex 1 Numbered unit — a specific physical bus with a timeline. Yes. It arrives somewhere, so it becomes supply for the next trip.
Ollex Ad hoc — “some bus from Ollex's yard”. A perfectly legitimate assignment. No. Nobody knows which bus it was or where it ended up, so it can never feed a next trip. The app calls this chain-invisible.
(empty) Demand — this trip needs a bus. —

3.3 A chain

A chain is one bus's sequence: arrive, rest a day or two, depart again, repeat. Each step is a link. The app plans chains many hops deep, because the second hop only exists once the first is taken.

3.4 Empty runs (repositioning)

If a bus is parked in Oslo and the only trip left departs Stockholm, someone has to drive it there with no passengers. That is an empty run — real cost, no revenue.

The app will propose one, but only as a last resort: every free link is placed before any link costing an empty run, and empty runs are always counted separately in every summary so you can weigh them. You can switch them off entirely (chapter 6.3).

3.5 Provisional versus committed

This is the distinction to keep straight:

Planned (provisional)Accepted (committed)
Where it livesRecomputed in memory every time anything changesStored in the database
In the export?NeverYes — written into Company name
Can the planner change it?Constantly — it re-plans after every editNo. A committed assignment is fixed supply the planner routes around
How to undo itNothing to undo — ignore itThe Undo button, or the master re-chain

So: an accepted assignment is safe from replanning without any locking. You never have to protect your decisions from the app.

3.6 Free days versus the gap

Everything on screen counts free days: whole days where the bus does nothing. A bus arriving on the 3rd and departing on the 7th has 3 free days (4th, 5th, 6th).

The rules internally use the gap — arrival day to departure day — which is always one larger. You will only meet the gap in the rule tables in chapter 4; every label, badge and sentence in the app is in free days.

4. The rulebook: when can a bus take a trip?

A bus may take a trip only when all of these pass. Every proposal in the app has passed all of them; every explanation of a refusal names the one that failed first.

4.1 Rule 1 — the time window

A bus that finishes on day D can start a new trip no sooner than the drive allows, and no later than three parked days after that. Same-day handovers are never valid.

Where the bus is vs. where it must beEarliestLatestWhy
Same city next day
(gap 1)		 4 days later
(gap 4)		 Overnight turnaround, then at most 3 days parked.
Near pair — CPH↔OSL, CPH↔STO, OSL↔STO, OSL↔BGO 2 days
(gap 2)		 5 days
(gap 5)		 One clear day covers the empty drive, then the same 3 days of rest.
Any other pair of cities 3 days
(gap 3)		 6 days
(gap 6)		 Two clear days to drive, then the same 3 days of rest.

The rest allowance is always the same 3 days — the window slides later when there is driving to do, it does not get wider. And the near-pair list is not transitive: CPH–BGO is two short hops via Oslo, but it is not itself a short hop.

Worked example

Kautra 3 finishes in CPH on 5 July. What can it take next?

Candidate tripVerdict
Departs CPH on 7 Jul✅ Same city, 1 free day. Ideal.
Departs CPH on 10 Jul❌ 4 free days parked — past the 3-day rest limit.
Departs STO on 7 Jul⚠️ Legal (near pair, 1 of the free days is the drive) but it is an empty run, so it ranks behind every free option.
Departs BGO on 7 Jul❌ Far pair needs 2 clear days to drive; there is only 1. The bus cannot physically get there in time.

4.2 Rule 2 — cities must match exactly

Matching is on canonical city codes from the registry. CPH, cph, CPH trf and CPH(trf) are all the same city — those are formatting differences the app is allowed to normalize. Anything the registry does not know is not a city, and the row says so rather than being guessed at.

4.3 Rule 3 — the company constraint (the Bus Preference column)

Bus Preference is the one passthrough-looking column that is actually a hard rule, and it is read as follows:

Cell containsMeaningShown in the Data tab as
(empty)Anyone may serve this trip.—
VilnedaOnly Vilneda may serve it — any of their buses.a blue must use badge
NO VilnedaVilneda may never serve it.an orange must avoid badge

The constraint is at company level: “only Vilneda” is satisfied by Vilneda 1, Vilneda 4, or an ad hoc Vilneda — but never by another operator. The name must resolve to a company in the registry; free text here fails the row, which is why the cell editor offers a closed list rather than a text box.

The ad hoc fallback: when a “must use X” trip has no chainable X bus at all, the app suggests the ad hoc X — “send some bus from their yard” — ranked last. That is rule 3's designed escape.

4.4 Rule 10 — party size (the Pax column)

Your chartered buses are 49-seaters. So a trip whose Pax says anything other than 49 — a party of 19, a party of 72 — is not work those buses can take, and the app will only ever put it on an ad hoc bus.

In practice: such a trip gets no chain suggestions, the planner and Optimize leave it alone, the board shows it as out of reach, and dragging it onto a numbered bus is refused with the reason spelled out (“the trip carries 19 pax, not 49 — only an ad hoc bus can take it”). Every company's ad hoc lane stays open, which is where the trip is meant to go. The rule is not about whether the people would physically fit: 19 is barred for the same reason 72 is.

An empty Pax cell is not a violation. Blank means unstated, and an unstated party size constrains nothing — pasted and hand-entered rows often have no Pax, and it would be wrong to quietly put them out of the fleet's reach.

Rows that arrive already breaking it. The rule governs what a bus may pick up, not what your sheet says it is already carrying. If an imported row puts a 55-pax trip on Ollex 1, the app leaves it alone, still treats that bus as available afterwards, and lists the row in the integrity report as pax_mismatch for you to decide about — the same treatment every other pre-existing problem gets.

4.5 Rule 6 — one bus cannot be in two places

Before proposing a link the app replays that bus's whole timeline: no overlap with an existing trip, no existing trip sitting inside the gap, and if the bus has a later trip already, that later trip must still be reachable afterwards. A proposal never breaks a bus's schedule.

Existing problems in your data — one trip sitting on top of another on the same bus — are listed in the integrity report, never silently fixed, and they do not freeze the rest of that bus's schedule. The commonest cause, a typo'd year stretching a trip across twelve months, no longer gets this far: those rows are refused into the non-operable list, where you can fix the date in place (chapters 5.2 and 5.3).

4.6 Empty runs only off the end of a chain

A bus may be driven empty only from the last trip on its timeline. Mid-chain it would arrive in the wrong city for whatever comes after, stranding it.

The cost of this rule is a blind spot: a bus can sit idle for weeks mid-timeline — a far-off committed trip pinning the end of its row — and the planner will propose nothing for it. Those blocks are enumerated in the Parked buses panel (chapter 7.9), where placing a trip into one is a decision you make, priced in empty-run days.

4.7 One new link per bus per planning round

When the planner builds a chain, each bus takes at most one new link per pass, then everything is re-validated. This is why a plan can look conservative: it is the rule that stops two overlapping trips landing on the same bus in one sweep.

4.8 Canceled trips

Canceled trips never appear in matching, never count against a fleet, and never occupy a bus. They are kept for audit, they can be shown on the board with a checkbox, and if a canceled trip was feeding a chain, everything downstream of it lands on the Worklist.

4.9 Pending trips are a risk, not a blocker

A Pending trip can be chained from and onto like any other, but every suggestion tells you how many Pending trips it depends on (“depends on 3 Pending trips” — hover it for the list). If one of them cancels, the chain below it breaks and appears on the Worklist.

4.10 Rule 11 — a hired bus can be sent away, up to three weeks out

A bare company name is a booking, not a vehicle: you paid someone to cover that trip. Such a booking can be cancelled up to 21 days before the trip starts — after that it stands, whatever else the fleet could do. This is the only rule in the app measured from today rather than between two trips.

What it buys you: an ad hoc trip is no longer a dead end. If one of your own coaches later comes free in the right city at the right time, the trip can be moved onto it and the hire called off — see 6.7 Reclaim from ad hoc…. The rule binds that button only; you can still clear a Company cell by hand whenever you like.

5. The Data tab

This is the sheet itself: what was imported, how the app read it, and every correction you make.

5.1 The two tables

Operable trips
Rows the app understood. These are the ones that can be planned. Rows whose start date is in the past are faded.
Non-operable rows
Rows that failed validation, each with a Reasons column listing every problem at once (not just the first). They are exported untouched, so nothing is lost by leaving them — but they cannot be assigned a bus until they are fixed.

5.2 What is checked, column by column

ColumnRule
StatusRequired. Exactly one of Confirmed, Pending, Canceled.
ProjectIf filled, must be numeric. Not unique — a big group split across three buses shares one project id.
Bus PreferenceIf filled, must resolve to a registry company, optionally prefixed NO . See 4.3.
Start date, End dateRequired. Real dates, start ≤ end, and no more than 60 days apart. Text that is not a date fails the row — it is never repaired or guessed. The 60-day rule catches typo'd years: real trips here run 0–11 days, so a row spanning a year is a wrong digit, not a long charter. Nothing is corrected — the row is listed with its span and you fix the date.
Departure, ArrivalRequired. Must resolve to a registry city code.
Company nameIf filled, must resolve to a registry company or one of its numbered units.
PaxNever fails a row, but read by rule 10 — see 4.4. Blank is fine (unstated).
ERRead only to label an empty run that is already visible in the data. Never used for matching, never written by the app. n/a, no, - and blank all mean “no ER”.
P Notes, Nationality, OP, Quoted rate, C NotesPassthrough. Never read for logic, preserved exactly. Nationality and OP get a dropdown from the registry purely so spellings stay tidy.

Everything to the right of these columns — the legacy date grid — is carried through the export untouched and is never maintained by the app.

5.3 Editing a cell

Click any cell to edit it. Enter saves, Esc cancels. Dates get a date field, Status and the registry-backed columns get a dropdown, free text gets a text box.

An edited cell is highlighted, and an edit is re-validated by exactly the same contract as an import — which means a row can move between the two tables as you type. Fixing the city code on a non-operable row makes it jump into the operable table, plannable immediately.

The Company name cell has a special picker with availability tiers, because it is the one cell that assigns a bus:

  • Buses — the engine suggests these — units the planner itself proposes for this trip. These passed every rule.
  • Buses — free on these dates — no clash, but not proposed either: the placement breaks a chaining rule the planner will not bend, or the bus is simply spent elsewhere in the plan.
  • Buses — busy on these dates — already carrying a trip that overlaps this one (hover to see which trip).
  • Ad hoc — company only, no unit (never chains) — the bare-company entries, in their own group because they never chain. A “must use X” trip with no chainable X bus highlights its mandated company here — rule 3's fallback.

Typing in that picker only filters the list; it never becomes the value, so a cell can never be rewritten to something nobody picked.

5.4 Adding trips

Add trip opens a form laid out in sheet order. Required fields are marked; everything else may be left empty. It is validated by the same contract, so a bad row lands in Non-operable with its reasons rather than being rejected at the form.

Manual rows are numbered for the sheet row they will occupy on export — 1672 — so the Row column matches the file you are about to send. They always number above the last row of the sheet, which is why they sit at the bottom until you sort by something else.

5.5 Pasting rows from the sheet

Paste rows takes rows copied straight out of Excel: one trip per line, tab separated, header optional.

The hidden-column trap. Excel silently leaves hidden columns out of what it copies, and nothing in the pasted text says so. Read against the full layout, every value after the gap lands one column to the left — and if the hidden columns sit after Arrival, the shifted row still validates. Validation cannot save you here. So the paste form has a tick list: untick the columns your sheet hides. The ticks are remembered by your browser. If your paste includes a header row, it wins over the ticks for that paste.

Dates are read as the sheet renders them (1-Apr-26) or as 2026-04-01. Anything genuinely ambiguous — 04/05/2026 — is kept exactly as typed so the contract can fail the row and quote it, rather than the app picking April or May for you.

After a paste the app reports which columns it read, which it left empty, and whether any row carried more fields than you declared. The whole paste is a single undo step.

5.6 Deleting rows

The × at the end of a row deletes it; ticking rows and pressing Delete selected deletes the batch as one undo step. A deleted row leaves the working set entirely and is dropped from the exported sheet. You are always asked to confirm — noticing you deleted the wrong row is the hard part, not reversing it.

5.7 Finding things: search, filter, sort, columns

  • Search matches any visible column. Hidden columns are deliberately not searched, so a row never appears for an invisible reason.
  • Filter row — a column with few distinct values gets a dropdown; an open-ended one gets a contains-match box. Status is the one multi-select: tick Confirmed and Pending to see “everything still live”.
  • Sort by clicking a header: ascending → descending → off. Off is meaningful — it restores the source file's own row order. Empty cells sort to the bottom whichever direction you pick: a blank means the value is missing, not that it is the smallest. Row sorts by row number, so pasted rows land after the sheet rows they will follow on export, and sorting Row ascending gives you the source order back. Hiding the column you are sorted by turns the sort off.
  • Columns hides columns you do not use; the setting is remembered by your browser. P Notes, Nationality, OP, Quoted rate and C Notes start hidden.
  • Today scrolls to the first trip starting today or later.
  • Ticks select rows for bulk delete. Shift-click ticks a whole range as displayed; the header box selects everything in the current view — which makes “filter to Canceled → select all → delete” the whole bulk-cleanup workflow.

Filters are never restored on load. A filter silently hiding rows at startup reads as data loss.

5.8 Undo

The ↶ button appears on Data, Suggestions and Timelines, and it is one shared history — an edit made on Data can be undone from Suggestions, because a change on one tab routinely invalidates work done on another. It names the action it will reverse.

  • 50 steps deep, per dataset. Importing replaces the dataset and starts a fresh history — nothing from before an import can be undone, so export anything you may still want before importing over it.
  • Batch actions (a paste, a bulk delete, an accepted chain, a re-chain, an applied optimization) are one step each.
  • There is no redo. Undo itself is not undoable.
  • An action that failed leaves no undo entry.

6. The Suggestions tab

The plan in words: what the fleet could run, grouped by bus, with the accept controls.

6.1 The summary line

Something like: “113 of 190 uncovered trips can be chained onto 24 units, costing 268 free days (31 of them driving an empty run, the rest idle).”

If it also says “N uncovered trips are theoretically reachable…”, that is answering the question the low number provokes: reachability ignores whether a bus is free. The plan's number is what the fleet can actually absorb; the difference needs capacity, not planning.

6.2 Objective: most trips covered, or fewest idle days

Switching replans immediately.

  • Most trips covered (default) prefers the trip that hands the bus back soonest, because that bus is the next link's supply — it maximizes the number of links.
  • Fewest idle days prefers the earliest start, minimizing days parked. Note that days spent driving an empty run are counted as worked, not idle — otherwise this objective would simply avoid distance rather than avoid waiting.

6.3 Empty runs: Allowed or Same city only

This single switch governs the whole engine — chains, one-hop candidates, board ghost slots and even the wording of the explanations.

With empty runs off, a bus can only take a trip departing from the city it last arrived in. That is a much smaller world: on the reference dataset it takes the plan from 113 links to 60, and most of the fleet ends up parked where nothing departs. If the plan comes back empty, the app says so explicitly rather than letting the setting look like a broken engine.

6.4 Plan ahead: how far out the planner decides

2 weeks, 1 month (the default), 3 months or All. The planner only proposes a bus for a trip that starts after today and no later than the date printed beside the buttons (“through …”). Switching replans immediately.

The limit is there because a planner with none ties buses to work months away, on a schedule that is still being edited.

Stays inside the windowIgnores it
The chain cards, the single-hop candidates, the candidate slots and Parked buses fits on the board, Optimize coverage…, Re-chain future… and Reclaim from ad hoc…. Anything you do by hand: dragging a bar on the board (chapter 7.6) and Assign by hand (7.7).

It limits which trips get a proposal, not which buses are available. A bus whose last trip ended before the window opens is standing there ready, and still counts.

Trips outside the window are not hidden. They are on the Data tab as always, the summary line counts them (“n more uncovered trips outside the plan-ahead window”), and with All uncovered ticked the board's Uncovered row draws them and counts them too. If the plan looks empty, read that count before concluding the fleet has no room.

A hired bus needs 21 days' notice to send away (6.7). At 2 weeks no trip is both far enough out to cancel and near enough to plan, so Reclaim from ad hoc… finds nothing, and its panel says so.

6.5 Accepting

Each chain card shows the bus, the number of trips, the free days (and how many of them are driving), and the number of empty runs. Underneath, one row per link.

ControlWhat it does
Accept this linkCommits the first link only. Later links say “needs the link above first” — their supply bus is not assigned yet, so there is nothing to chain from.
Accept chain (n)Commits that whole chain, front to back, in one undo step.
Accept entire plan (n)Commits every chain. You are asked to confirm, and told how many empty runs you are buying.
“n other buses could take it”Opens that trip on the board so you can compare and pick a different bus.

Accepting anything re-plans everything else around it — the rest of the proposal will change, and that is normal. Only what you accepted is written.

6.6 Re-chain future… — the only thing that takes assignments back

Everything else in the app is additive. Re-chain releases assignments the app made and rebuilds them from scratch under the current objective, empty-run and plan-ahead settings.

Five boundaries decide what may be released, and none is negotiable:

  1. Only assignments this app wrote into a cell that was blank on import. A company name that came from the spreadsheet is source data — change it on the Data tab or in Excel.
  2. Only trips that have not started yet.
  3. Never a locked trip.
  4. Only numbered buses. Sending a hired-in (ad hoc) bus away is a cancellation, not a rearrangement — that is Reclaim from ad hoc… below.
  5. Only trips inside the plan-ahead window (6.4). The replan cannot see past it, so a trip released beyond the cutoff could never get its bus back. Widen the window first if you want those re-decided.

The panel states how many assignments each rule froze, so a small releasable number against hundreds of future trips reads as the boundaries working rather than as a bug.

Read the stranded count before applying. Releasing N assignments does not give N back: the replan may spend a bus on a trip the old arrangement left uncovered and strand the trip it took the bus from. The panel names those trips (click one to see it on the board) and states the net change first. Measured on the reference data: with the coverage objective and empty runs allowed, 46 → 113 covered and nothing stranded; with the idle objective and empty runs off, 18 of 60 released trips came back with no bus at all.

Nothing is written until you press Apply, and applying is a single undo step.

6.7 Reclaim from ad hoc… — take work back off hired buses

A bare company name (Ollex rather than Ollex 1) means a bus hired in for that one trip. Such a trip is covered, so the planner leaves it alone forever — even when one of your own coaches later turns out to be free and standing in the right city. Reclaim from ad hoc is the button that looks for exactly those.

A hire can be sent away up to 21 days before the trip starts; inside that window it stands, and the panel says how many were frozen for that reason. Two more are never offered: a locked trip, and one whose Pax is not 49 — rule 10 means no chartered coach could take it, so cancelling the hire could only leave it worse off.

It can never leave a trip without a bus. A hire is cancelled only when a chartered coach is being written in its place; every other eligible trip keeps the bus it already had, and the panel says how many. That is the difference from a re-chain, which can strand a trip.

The one place the app may overwrite the spreadsheet. This action will cancel a hire the sheet itself named — everywhere else, a company name that came in on the file is left alone. The reasoning: a bare company name records that somebody booked a bus, and a booking inside its notice period can be called off. It is a proposal either way — nothing is written until you press Apply, and the panel tells you how many of the hires came from the spreadsheet. The deadline binds this button, not you: you can still clear the Company cell on the Data tab, or drag the bar to the Uncovered lane (chapter 7.6), at any time.

The panel also names the trips the current plan proposes and this one does not. Those are not losses — they have no bus today either, and the buses went to the reclaimed trips instead. On the reference data the reclaim covers more overall (113 → 122 trips), because a reclaimed trip becomes a starting point for the next link. Applying is a single undo step, which puts every cancelled hire back exactly as it was.

6.8 Optimize coverage… — the exact solver

The ordinary plan is built greedily, one best-looking link at a time. Optimize coverage hands the same rules to an exact solver which considers the whole future at once. It takes a few seconds, so it is an on-demand preview rather than something recomputed on every edit.

The panel says how many trips it covers, how many the greedy plan covers, and how many empty runs the difference costs. On the reference data: 124 covered against the greedy 113 — bought with roughly 12 more empty runs. Since the model has no distances or rates, it cannot price those drives; that trade is yours to make, which is why it is a preview.

Every link it proposes is replayed through the ordinary rule check before it reaches the screen; anything that fails is dropped and counted. There are no per-link accept buttons here — the point of the plan is that it was solved together, so it is applied whole.

The solver is an optional extra (buschain[solver]). If it is not installed the button reports that rather than failing quietly; everything else works unchanged.

6.9 Single-hop candidates

The collapsed section at the bottom lists only the next link, ungrouped, in either direction: per uncovered trip (“who could take this?”) or per free unit (“what could this bus take?”). It is the raw ranked list — no chains, no contention — and it is where the ad hoc fallback for a “must use X” trip appears.

7. The Timelines board

The same information as a calendar: one column per day, one row per bus. This is where most of the actual work gets done, because it is the only view where you can see why a bus is idle.

7.1 The rows

  1. Uncovered — the work queue, at the top. By default it shows only the trips the planner can act on today; the row label counts what is being left out, and All uncovered shows everything. (If nothing is actionable, they are all shown anyway — otherwise there would be nothing to click.)
  2. Units — one row per numbered bus, in registry order.
  3. Ad hoc (chain-invisible) — one row per company, for assignments that name no specific bus. Toggle with the Ad hoc checkbox.

Show plan on buses decides where the plan's proposals are drawn. Unticked, a planned trip stays in the Uncovered row. Ticked, it sits on the row of the bus that would run it, so the board reads as a proposed schedule. Either way the bar is hatched, nothing has been written, and the accept controls are the same: the box changes the picture, not the plan. Unlike the other three boxes, it is remembered the next time you open the app.

Bars that would overlap on the same bus are stacked rather than drawn side by side — that stacking is how a double-booking becomes visible without reading any dates.

7.2 The colours

Colour is always the status, in every lane of the board: green Confirmed, yellow Pending, red Canceled. A trip does not change colour because of where it sits — an uncovered Confirmed trip is the same green as a Confirmed trip already on a bus. Lanes differ a little in strength (the uncovered lane is turned up, the ad hoc lane down) but never in hue.

7.3 Getting around

  • Zoom S / M / L — day width. Your position in time is kept.
  • Today — jumps to today's line.
  • Show canceled — folds canceled trips back in as history.
  • Click a bar to select it; Esc clears; d jumps to that trip's row on the Data tab (clearing the Data filters if they were hiding it, and saying so).
  • Drag a bar to move it — sideways for its dates, up or down for a different bus (7.6).
  • Click an empty part of the board to deselect.

7.4 Selecting an uncovered trip

The panel answers “who could take this?”, in this order:

  • If the plan already routes it: which bus, after which trip, how many free days, which step of which chain — plus the other buses that could take it instead, so the plan's pick reads as one option and not a verdict.
  • If there are candidates: the ranked list with Accept buttons.
  • If there are none: a plain-language reason — “No unit arrives in STO. The closest empty run — Kautra 3 from CPH — needs 1 clear day to drive over and misses by 2 days.” — followed by whether a chain could ever reach the trip or not. Those two facts lead to different actions, so they are stated separately.
  • If the planner is not looking at the trip at all — it has already started, or it starts after the plan-ahead window — the panel says exactly that and stops. No bus was asked, so there is no rule to quote, and nothing here means you need to hire one.
  • Always, at the bottom: Assign by hand, outside the plan — for a trip that has already left, this is how you record which bus really ran it.

7.5 Selecting an assigned trip

The panel answers the opposite question: what can this bus take next? A chain can only ever be extended from its last trip, so the list is always measured from there (and says so if you selected a mid-chain trip).

Engine-legal options keep their ordinary Accept button. Below them is the override list, in two groups that are deliberately never blended:

  • “The dates work — it is the company constraint that refuses these”.
  • “Nearest on the calendar”, closest miss first.

Every row names the rule it breaks, both as a pill and inside the button: Assign anyway · 2d too soon, Assign anyway · 3d past rest, Assign anyway · against constraint. Ten are shown at a time with a “show all” link.

7.6 Moving a trip by dragging it

Every bar on the board can be picked up. The first few pixels of movement decide what the drag means, and the two gestures never mix:

  • Sideways moves the trip's dates. A preview follows the bar (“+3d — moves the trip's dates in the sheet; the span is unchanged”), and dropping writes Start and End together as one edit — one undo step. It is the same change as typing new dates on the Data tab, made where you can see the neighbours it has to fit between.
  • Up or down moves the trip to another bus. Every row that may legally take it lights up. Hold the bar over a row and the note under the pointer says what the drop would do — chains after which trip on how many free days, needs an empty run to get there first, lands first on this bus, or leaves the bus parked further than a chain reaches. A row that refuses says why, in the engine's own words.

Three landing places behave differently:

  • A bus's row assigns the trip to that bus. When the placement is a rule-1 handover, the drop records the chain link too — the server rechecks the rules and derives the link itself, so a stale board can never write a stale chain. A drop further out than a chain reaches records the assignment alone, with no connector.
  • A company's ad hoc lane books “some further bus from their yard”: it chains to nothing and generates no supply.
  • The Uncovered row takes the bus off the trip. If the bus came from the spreadsheet, the note warns you first: the export will carry a blank where the sheet named one.

The drag refuses the impossible; the hatches allow the merely unwise. A drop is offered only where no hard rule breaks — a bus running two trips at once, a departure it cannot physically reach, a company or party-size constraint. For deliberate overrides — “I know the window is blown, do it anyway” — use the escape hatches in 7.7, which exist precisely for that.

Esc cancels a drag in progress and the bar goes back where it was.

7.7 Assigning by hand (the two escape hatches)

The two pickers — Assign by hand on an uncovered trip's panel (bus-for-a-trip) and the override lists on a bus's panel (trip-for-a-bus, 7.5) — are deliberately not filtered to legal options. When the planner has nothing to offer is exactly when a human needs to place a bus, and an app that refuses to let you say what you have decided is worse than one that records it and reports the consequences. That is the opposite stance from the drag (7.6), which offers only legal landings: the drag is for routine moves, the hatches for deliberate overrides.

So a hand assignment from either panel:

  • is written immediately and appears in the export;
  • records no chain link, so no connector is drawn — unlike a drag that lands as a rule-1 handover, which records one;
  • shows up in the integrity findings as a gap out of window or a city mismatch, rather than being blocked;
  • is a single undo step.

After assigning from a bus's panel, the selection follows onto the trip you just assigned — so you can keep extending the same bus without leaving the board.

7.8 Locking a trip

The Lock button on the selected trip's panel means “do not re-decide this”:

  • It leaves the demand set — no suggestions, no plan links, nothing to accept.
  • A master re-chain refuses to release it.
  • It keeps its place on its bus's timeline and still feeds the next hop. A lock pins a trip; it does not park the bus.
  • It is stored separately from your sheet data, so it never appears in the export. Lock and unlock step back with Undo like anything else.

Locked trips are always drawn on the board even when the “actionable only” default would hide them — otherwise locking would be a one-way door, since unlocking happens on the panel you reach by clicking the bar.

7.9 What the banners and panels below the board tell you

Capacity note
“Peak 2026-07-18: 31 uncovered trips run at once, against a registered fleet of 24 buses.” Chaining moves buses between trips; it cannot conjure one. When simultaneous demand exceeds the whole fleet, the shortfall needs capacity, not planning. If some of your buses are stranded, the note says that too — that part is recoverable by scheduling. It names each one with where it is parked and its nearest miss, e.g. “Nordibuzz 1 (in STO since Nov 3; nearest trip, CPH→OSL on Nov 6, is 1 day too soon to drive there)”. Hover a name for the full reason (7.10); click it to open that bus's panel and see what it could take next.
Fleet over-commitment warnings
Per company per day, more simultaneous trips on numbered buses than that company has buses. Ad hoc assignments are deliberately excluded: they mean “a further bus from their yard”, which the charter count says nothing about (counting them produced 1 448 warning-days on the reference data and buried the real problems). An empty panel is the normal state.
Unit integrity findings
Every consecutive pair on every bus, classified. Click one to jump to it on the board.
  • pax_mismatch — a trip for a party other than 49 sitting on a numbered bus. The one finding about a single trip rather than a pair, so it names one row; only an imported sheet can produce it (chapter 4.4).
  • overlap — the same bus on two trips at once. A real problem.
  • gap_out_of_window — too soon or too long a rest between two trips.
  • city_mismatch — the bus ends somewhere other than where the next trip starts, with nothing explaining it.
  • explicit_er — the same thing, but your sheet's ER column says it was intended. An enumerated fact, not a violation.
  • reposition — an empty run you approved here. Also a fact, listed as a cost worth seeing.
Findings are always listed, never auto-fixed.
Parked buses
Buses idle mid-timeline for longer than any chain window — usually a far-off committed trip pinning the end of the row. The planner cannot propose into these blocks (empty runs only run off the end of a chain, 4.6), so the panel lists what a person could do instead: every uncovered trip that would fit, cheapest first, each badged with the empty driving it costs (“2 days empty”, or “no drive” when the trip departs from where the bus already stands) and how many days after the bus lands it departs. A fit deeper than the chain window adds “records no link” — placing it is an assignment, not a chain. Click a fit to see the trip on the board; placing it is the ordinary drag (7.6). Windows with nothing to offer are still listed — a bus parked for six weeks is worth knowing about even when no trip fits.

7.10 Why is this bus idle?

Hover a unit's row label. Marked rows carry a diagnosis, and the distinction worth knowing is:

  • done — its chain runs to the end and no uncovered trip starts after that. Not idle; finished.
  • booked — trips could be reached in time, but each is ruled out by a company constraint or clashes with what it is already booked for.
  • stranded — parked where nothing it can reach departs from. Because a chain only extends from its tail, no accept in any order will put this bus back to work. The tooltip names the closest misses on both sides of the window, e.g. “the soonest it could aim for is CPH→OSL on 12 Aug, but driving empty STO→CPH needs 1 clear day and there is only 1 free day before it — short by 1 day.”

With empty runs switched off, a stranded bus is usually stranded by the setting, and the tooltip says exactly that.

8. The Worklist tab

One list: broken chains. An assignment lands here when the trip it was chained from has since been canceled or unassigned — the bus that was supposed to arrive and hand over is no longer arriving.

It is transitive. Cancel one trip and everything downstream of it on that bus appears, not just the next link.

Unassign & re-suggest frees the trip so the planner proposes it again. When the assignment came from your source file the button says so instead — Clear sheet's bus & re-suggest — and spells out the consequence: the export will carry a blank Company cell where the sheet named a bus. Undo reverses it either way. (Automation is held to a stricter rule: a re-chain only ever releases assignments the app itself wrote.)

“No broken chains” is the normal, healthy state.

9. The Registry tab

The app's list of what exists: companies, their buses, and valid city codes. Every Company name, Bus Preference, Departure and Arrival value has to resolve here — which is why this tab decides how many of your rows are operable at all.

9.1 Seeding

Seed from Information sheet is a one-time action per registry: it reads the imported workbook's Information sheet and builds companies, units and cities. After that the registry is maintained here. Warnings from the seed — and from every later import — stay on screen; read them.

9.2 Companies and units

  • Add / update creates a company or updates its note.
  • Rename changes a company's name everywhere at once — use it when a company actually changes its name, not to fix which company a trip is on. Every trip naming it is rewritten, so Ollex 1 becomes Ollez 1 in the Data table, on the board, in Suggestions and in the Worklist, and a NO Ollex preference becomes NO Ollez. Units and the note are kept. Nothing stops resolving and no row becomes non-operable, which is the reason the rename touches the trips at all rather than the registry alone. One Undo reverses all of it.
  • + unit adds a numbered bus; the × on a chip removes one. Removal is refused while live trips still name that unit — the refusal lists them (up to five) — because a unit that stops resolving would silently drop its rows out of the operable set. Reassign those trips first.
  • Fleet is not editable, because it is simply the number of units. One number cannot disagree with itself — change the fleet by adding or removing buses. (The sheet's Fleets column still owns the value: it sets the unit list on import.)
  • Delete removes a company — every trip referencing it becomes non-operable, so you are asked to confirm.

A company with no row in the Information sheet's Bus Companies column gets a warning: its fleet count cannot be written back on export.

Two things to expect after a rename. The exported workbook carries the new name, including in rows the app had never touched before, so that export is no longer byte-identical to the file you imported — that is the rename doing its job, and re-importing that export is what keeps everything agreeing. And free text is left alone: a note that happens to mention the old name still says it, because rewriting prose is guesswork the app does not do. Edit those by hand if they matter.

9.3 Cities

A closed list of canonical codes. Add the code exactly as the sheet spells it. Case and the trf suffix are handled for you; anything else is a different city.

The list lives in the workbook, in the Information sheet's Cities column: an import reads it, an export writes the current list back, creating the column if your workbook has not got one yet. A workbook that heads no such column leaves the registry alone — saying nothing is not the same as saying “no cities”.

One difference from the two pick lists below, and it matters: trips are validated against these codes. So if you delete a city in Excel and re-import, the app will not silently drop it while live trips still depart from or arrive there — it keeps the code and tells you. Removing it for real is done here, where the app first lists the trips that would be affected.

9.4 Nationality and OP pick lists

These two are dropdown sources only — the contract never validates against them, so pruning a typo out of the menu can never knock a row out of the operable set. They are not app-only state either: they live in the workbook (Information columns O and P), an import reads them back, and an export writes the current list out.

10. Exporting, and what the file contains

10.1 Export xlsx

Downloads buschain-export.xlsx: your original workbook with only the cells this app wrote patched into it. Concretely that means:

  • accepted assignments in Company name;
  • any cell you edited in the app, in its own column;
  • trips you added, appended as new rows;
  • rows you deleted, dropped;
  • fleet counts and the Cities / Nationality / OP list columns on the Information sheet, if they differ from what the sheet holds;
  • a company rename, carried into every row that names the company and into the Information sheet's company column (chapter 9.2).

Everything else — formatting, comments, formulas, the legacy grid, non-operable rows — is byte-for-byte what you imported. Provisional plans, locks and previews are never in the file.

The one non-identical export. If your workbook predates the Cities / Nationality / OP list columns, the first export creates them (headers included) on the Information sheet. Every export after that is byte-identical again, because the file is then a fixed point.

10.2 Export csv

The same data flattened to csv. Useful for a quick diff or for a system that does not read xlsx. A csv-sourced dataset can also be exported as a freshly built workbook.

10.3 The safe rhythm

Import → work → export → send. The exported file is the deliverable; the database is your working state. If you re-import a newer version of the planner, the old working dataset is replaced, so export first.

Replaced does not mean instantly gone: the app keeps the last five imported workbooks so a file can be recovered if you lose the original, and drops anything older each time you import. There is nothing to clean up and the database does not grow without limit — but the retained copies are the file as it arrived, not your work on it, so an export you never took is still an export you no longer have.

11. Recipes: how the common jobs are done

11.1 A normal planning session

  1. Import the latest planner; check the operable / non-operable counts.
  2. Fix non-operable rows (11.2) — every one you fix is a trip that can be planned.
  3. On Suggestions, check the objective, the empty-run setting and Plan ahead are what you want.
  4. Work down the chain cards. Accept the ones you agree with; for a trip you want on a different bus, use the “n other buses could take it” link.
  5. Switch to Timelines and look at the leftovers: the Uncovered row and the stranded units.
  6. Place what the planner could not, by hand (11.4).
  7. Check Fleet warnings and Integrity findings.
  8. Export.

11.2 Fix a non-operable row

  1. Data tab → the lower table. Read the Reasons cell; it lists every problem at once.
  2. Click the offending cell and correct it.
  3. If the reason is “not a registry city code” or “does not resolve to a registry company”, the fix may belong on the Registry tab instead — the value may be right and the registry incomplete.
  4. When the last reason clears, the row jumps to the operable table by itself.

11.3 Cover a trip nobody is proposing a bus for

  1. Timelines → click the trip's bar. Read the explanation.
  2. If it says a chain could still reach it, accept the earlier links first — it is waiting, not blocked.
  3. If empty runs are off, try switching them on: a bus may be one drive away.
  4. If it is genuinely out of reach, use Assign by hand — an ad hoc company assignment is often the right answer here.

11.4 Give a specific bus a specific trip

  1. Timelines → click that bus's last trip (or its row label).
  2. Find the trip in the list. Engine-legal ones have Accept; the rest have Assign anyway with the rule they break named on the button.
  3. Assign. The selection follows onto the new trip, ready for the next hop.

Or, from the trip's side: click the uncovered trip → Assign by hand → pick the bus.

11.5 Handle a cancellation

  1. Data tab → set that row's Status to Canceled.
  2. Worklist tab → anything that was chained off it is now listed.
  3. Unassign & re-suggest on each, so the planner can propose those trips again.
  4. Suggestions tab → accept the replacements.

11.6 Replan the whole future

  1. Set the objective, the empty-run switch and Plan ahead to what you want the future to look like. A re-chain never reaches past the plan-ahead window.
  2. Re-chain future… → read the preview: net change first, stranded trips, and what was frozen.
  3. Apply, or Discard. Applying is one undo step.

11.7 Squeeze out the maximum coverage

  1. Optimize coverage… and wait a few seconds.
  2. Compare: covered trips against the greedy plan, and the empty runs it costs.
  3. If the extra drives are worth it, Apply. If not, Discard — the greedy plan is still there.

11.8 Protect a decision from a future re-chain

You usually do not need to: a committed assignment is already immune to ordinary replanning. Lock a trip only when you want a master re-chain to leave it alone as well — a customer promise, a driver already booked.

12. Troubleshooting and FAQ

Every row is non-operable.
The registry is not seeded. Press Seed from Information sheet (chapter 2.3). Without it, no city and no company resolves.
The plan is empty and there are plenty of uncovered trips.
Check the empty-run switch first — with “Same city only”, most of the fleet is parked where nothing departs. Otherwise it means every uncovered trip needs a bus that is already committed elsewhere; the summary says which case it is.
The plan is empty, or stops short, and the summary says trips have “already started”.
The planner leaves a trip alone from its start date on — it will not propose a bus for something that has left. It also only ever continues a chain from a bus's recorded trips, with a few days' reach. So if trips went out and the sheet was never told which bus took them, the records stop short of everything still ahead and there is nothing to chain from: the fleet is not busy, it is unaccounted for. Select those trips and use Assign by hand (or type the bus into the Company cell on the Data tab). Each one you record gives the planner a bus in a known place, and the plan picks up from there.
The plan covers far fewer trips than are “reachable”.
Reachability ignores contention — it answers “could a chain ever get here?”, not “is a bus free?”. The plan's number is the real one. The gap is a capacity shortfall, not a planning failure.
One trip gets no suggestions at all, and no bus will accept it.
Check its Pax. Anything other than 49 means only an ad hoc bus may take it (chapter 4.4) — drag it onto a company's ad hoc lane. Failing that, its Bus Preference may name a company whose buses are all committed.
A bus stops halfway across the board and never takes another trip.
Hover its row label. It is done, booked or stranded — and if it is stranded, no order of accepts will help (chapter 7.10).
Can I take a bus off a trip the spreadsheet assigned?
Yes — you, deliberately: clear the Company cell on the Data tab, use the Worklist's Clear sheet's bus & re-suggest, or drag the bar to the Uncovered lane (chapter 7.6). The export then carries a blank where the sheet named a bus, and Undo puts it back. What may not do this is automation: a re-chain only ever releases assignments the app itself wrote.
Re-chain says it will only release a fraction of the future trips.
That is the five release boundaries doing their job — source-file assignments, trips already started, locked trips, ad hoc bookings (those are Reclaim's business, chapter 6.7) and anything starting after the plan-ahead window (chapter 6.4) are all frozen. The panel breaks the number down.
I applied a re-chain and coverage went down.
Coverage is not monotone under a release: the replan can spend a bus on a trip the old arrangement left out and strand the one it took the bus from. That is what the stranded list in the preview is for. Undo reverses the whole thing in one step.
A trip I expected is in the non-operable list, saying it spans too many days.
Its two dates are more than 60 days apart, which here almost always means a typo'd year in one of them — the reference workbook alone contains 76 such rows, mostly a start year one year early. Nothing has been changed: the dates are exactly as the sheet has them, and the row exports untouched. Fix the year in the row's date cell and it returns to the operable list immediately. If you genuinely run trips longer than two months, that limit is `MAX_TRIP_SPAN_DAYS` in contract.py.
My pasted rows came in shifted / mostly non-operable.
Hidden columns in Excel (chapter 5.5). Untick them in the paste form and paste again, or include the header row.
A dropdown does not offer a company I use.
Add it on the Registry tab. Pick lists there are live: a company added there is pickable immediately, without reloading.
Where did my provisional plan go?
Any change at all — an edit, an accept, a setting — re-derives it, and any open preview (re-chain, optimize) is dropped rather than shown stale. Nothing was lost; the plan is recomputed from scratch every time.
Does the app change my file while I work?
No. It never writes to your workbook at all. Your working state lives in the app's own database and only leaves it when you press Export.
Can two people use it at once?
It is built for one dispatcher on one machine. There are no accounts and no conflict handling.

13.3 Glossary

Ad hoc
An assignment naming a company but no bus. Legitimate, but chain-invisible: it is never supply and never sits on a timeline.
Chain
One bus's sequence of trips: arrive, rest, depart, repeat.
Demand
A live trip with no bus.
Empty run / repositioning
Driving a bus with no passengers to another city so it can start its next trip.
ER
The sheet's own empty-run mark. Read only to label an integrity finding; never written, never used for matching.
Free days
Whole days a bus does nothing between two trips. What every number on screen counts.
Gap
Arrival day to departure day — always one more than the free days. Used by the rules, not on screen.
Idle days
The “fewest idle days” objective's measure: parked days only, excluding days spent driving an empty run.
Link
One accepted (or proposed) step of a chain: this trip, then that one, on this bus.
Locked
A trip the planner must not re-decide. It stays on its timeline and still feeds the next hop.
Non-operable
A row that failed the data contract. Listed with reasons, exported untouched, not plannable until fixed.
Numbered unit
A physical bus, e.g. Ollex 1. The only thing that chains.
Parked window
A stretch where a bus sits idle between two committed trips for longer than any chain window. Invisible to the planner (empty runs are tail-only), so listed in the Parked buses panel instead.
Provisional
Computed, shown, never stored and never exported.
Reachable
Some sequence of accepts could chain into this trip without any empty run. Ignores whether a bus is free.
Stranded
A bus parked where nothing it can reach departs from, so no accept in any order can put it back to work.
Supply
The arrival of a trip assigned to a numbered unit: a bus, in a city, from a date.
